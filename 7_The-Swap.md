# The Swap

Nothing is opened in the yard. Replacement means the old vault leaves as one piece and a new one sits in the same hole. The price of that leaving is part of the replacement, not a bill the household discovers decades later.

---

## The day it comes out

On a house that already has a station, the house stays on the **utility** while the crew digs. Digging can take a day or two. The lights stay on. That is not the dark window.

A licensed crew then isolates the station from the house: gateway open, heat loop stopped, inverter off. They put the **shutdown locks back in** the same way Chapter 5 put them in at the factory. The vault stops making heat on purpose. Leftover heat is already small. They wait the hours it takes for that to fall further if the procedure says to wait.

Then a crane lifts the vault, still sealed, onto a permitted truck. The hole that is left is a known shape. The next vault, locked and cold, goes in. Chapter 4’s chase lands again in the utility room. Inspectors sign. A licensed startup is the first heat from the new unit.

The homeowner is without the station, and without house power only if the crew has to open the panel, for the **hours** of that lock, lift, set, and reconnect. The wall battery can cover part of that window. A first install on a new house can take days. That is a construction week. The house can stay on the utility while the crew works.

The homeowner does not store, cut, or dispose of anything. There is no pile of old parts by the garage. The core leaves with the vault, on that same truck.

If a unit is swapped early (damage, a recall, a sale that wants a newer vault), the day looks the same. Early or late, it is a crane event.

---

## What happens to the old vault

The truck goes to a licensed facility, not to the town dump.

**The box.** Lead, steel, and concrete are recycled or reused where that is possible. The vault was almost all shield and box. That mass is ordinary industrial material once it is clean enough for the facility’s rules.

**The fuel.** The work began as about **30 kilograms (66 pounds) at first start** (Chapter 8). Chapter 9 is what that heavy metal is (TRISO-UCO particles in graphite) and what is left in them after a century. After a century, what cannot be recycled or reused is still in that size class. It goes into a paint-can receptacle at a licensed site. It does not stay on the property or in the hole.

The owner never takes title to a waste drum. Take-back is the factory’s (or the licensed operator’s) job, paid as part of the swap.

---

## What the check includes

A replacement is sold as a **swap**:

- new vault
- removal of the old one
- haul
- processing (recycle what you can, receptacle for what you cannot)
- reconnect of power and heat in the utility room
- licensed shutdown and licensed startup

It should not be a surprise line item after someone has lived with the appliance for decades.

How that is paid can be any of these, or a mix:

- **Prepaid at first install.** A take-back fee sits in the original price, like a deposit on a tank.
- **Included core-exchange.** The company that sold the vault is on the hook to swap it. The homeowner pays a posted swap price that already assumes take-back.
- **A posted price at the time of swap.** Clear dollars, known in advance as a class of job, not a custom demolition bid.

Which of those wins is a business detail. The rule is the same: **replacement means the old station leaves, and the cost of that leaving is inside the replacement.**

The hole, the crane path, and the chase were supposed to stay usable (Chapter 4). If a later owner built a garage on the lift path, that is their extra bill. The vault is still lifted out as one piece. It is not cut open in the yard.

---

## What is not the swap

**Batteries, the inverter, and the Stirling units** die on a much shorter clock, often 10 to 15 years. Replacing those is a utility-room job, like solar or a furnace. It is not a crane day and not Chapter 7.

**The outdoor radiator or a house pump** is ordinary hardware. Replacing it is not a vault event.

**A playset or a patio** on the ten feet of soil is fine if footings stay shallow (Chapter 3). Do not drive long posts into the lid. That makes the later crane lift harder.

**The leftover fuel is small, and the process is still specialized.** It is not handled like a stack of old solar panels.

---

## A century, or sooner

The vault is designed to run for on the order of a hundred years without a hatch. That is a design life, not a promise that the first owner’s great-grandchildren must keep the same serial number. A swap can happen when the unit is done, when a better vault exists, or when the house is reworked.

Until that day, there is no refueling truck and the vault stays sealed. After that day, the story is one lift, one new vault, and leftover fuel in a paint-can-class receptacle at a licensed site.

## What this chapter covered

This is the end: locks in, crane out, same hole, recycle the box, leftover fuel to a licensed site, and a replacement price that already includes leaving. An existing house stays on the utility while the crew digs. The dark window, if there is one, is hours. Chapters 3 to 6 are how the yard, the house, the vault, and the stamp make that day ordinary. Chapter 9 is what is inside the paint-can that leaves on the truck. The household does not keep any part of the old vault.
