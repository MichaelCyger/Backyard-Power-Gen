# The Vault

The vault is the sealed box in the hole. Chapter 3 is why the lawn stays a lawn. Chapter 4 is how power and heat leave through the floor. This chapter is what is inside, why it can sit closed for on the order of a century, and why it is not a 1,000 MW plant with the roof taken off.

---

## What the box is

From the outside the vault is a lead-lined concrete object about the size of a large septic tank. A crane can lift it. Nobody on the lot opens it. The lid has no hatch for the owner. Pipes and cables leave through the bottom only.

Inside, in layers:

- **A small core** at the center: a beer-keg-to-dishwasher-sized assembly that holds the fuel
- **A hydrogen-rich and boron-bearing layer** that slows and catches neutrons (the particles that do not care much about lead)
- **Lead and thick concrete** that stop gammas (the hard X-ray-like radiation)
- **The sealed outer vault** the crane sees

The dirt above the lid is extra. The vault is sized to do the stopping on its own, including in dry soil. Chapter 3 is that argument.

## How it makes power

The core is a very small nuclear core. Heat comes from splitting atoms in a sealed fuel, at a scale closer to a large home furnace than to a commercial station.

That heat does two jobs at the same time. A share becomes electricity for the panel. The rest is warmth for floors, showers, or a greenhouse. Using both is the design. If the house or a greenhouse uses the warmth, most of what the core makes is used, not thrown away.

A house-scale electrical output of **5 to 10 kWe** (kilowatts of electricity) goes with about **20 to 40 kW of heat** from the core. That is furnace scale. There is no cooling tower. After shutdown, leftover heat drops to about as much as a small space heater, then lower over hours and days. Dirt, concrete, and time can take that.

Heat moves without the kind of pumps that wear out in a few years: heat pipes, or a still loop that circulates because hot fluid rises. Electricity is made by a long-life converter on that heat (a solid-state stack, or a sealed engine built for decades, not a steam turbine from a campus plant). The converter can live in the vault or at the house end of the chase. Either way, the owner does not service it in the yard.

The core is run **cold and slow**: a trickle of heat in a lot of mass. That is how a sealed life on the order of a century is even thinkable. The neutron battering that ages metal in a big plant is much gentler here because the power in each liter of core is small.

## The paint-can of fuel

Fuel use at this output, over a century, is measured in kilograms, not tons. Chapter 8 works the inventory: about **15 to 25 kilograms burned**, about **30 kilograms (about 66 pounds) loaded**, still a paint-can of heavy metal inside the core, inside the vault. The vault is almost all shield and box. The crane is for the box.

Material mixed into the fuel is used up as the fuel is used up, so the output stays even. There is no refueling truck. There is no hatch. At the end, what cannot be recycled is still in that paint-can size class at a licensed site (Chapter 7).

The fuel is assembled in the factory, in a shutdown state, and never handled on the lot.

## Why it stays put

A commercial plant needs staff, pumps, and a tower because leftover heat after shutdown is enormous. Here leftover heat is a space heater. The earth is the sink. The vault does not rely on a pump staying on.

If power is pulled faster or slower, the battery and the inverter on the house side take the jump. The core is not asked to chase the dryer. It makes a steady trickle.

If the core ever tries to run away (a sudden rise in splitting), the fuel and the geometry are chosen so heat itself slows the reaction: hotter means less splitting, in milliseconds, without a person or a motor. That is a materials fact, not a software mode.

If the vault ever gets far hotter than it should anyway, a passive layer above the core is meant to melt, flow, and set into a glass-like mass that stills the reaction and locks the insides in place. The whole vault stays one object. That is a last-ditch behavior. It is not something expected to happen in the life of the unit. The ordinary case is the small leftover heat and the earth.

None of that is a weekly inspection. None of it is an app.

## Off on the truck, on in the hole

Fresh fuel has never been used, so it is not already hot. In the factory the core is assembled with **locks and control elements in the shutdown position**. The vault is sealed around that. What leaves the plant is a heavy, closed object that is not producing electricity and not producing heat.

It is trucked as permitted heavy freight. A crane sets it. Chapter 4’s crew connects the house. Inspectors sign.

Only after it is buried and the chase is closed does a **licensed startup** happen. The shutdown locks are released in a controlled procedure. That is the first time the core makes heat. Until that step there is no running-plant dose for a neighbor, and nothing to cool.

At the end of life the same locks go back in before the crane returns. The vault that rides out is shut down on purpose, the same way it rode in.

The owner does not hold the key to those locks. The phone, if there is one, does not move them. Chapter 2’s optional storm setting is a battery hold on the house wall.

## What the vault is not

It is not a mini campus. No tower, no staff gate, no refueling floor.

It is not a thing you open to change a filter.

It is not safe because the dirt is deep. It is safe because the box is thick, the holes point down, the power is furnace-scale, and the locks stay in until a licensed person says otherwise.

It is not a 1,000 MW station made small by wish. It is a 20 to 40 kW heat source in a factory-sealed vault. That size is the whole approval argument (Chapter 6).
