No regulator has issued this approval yet. This chapter is the path.

The aim is not to site a 1,000 MW campus behind a house. The aim is to treat a factory-sealed, never-opened, furnace-scale vault as a **manufactured appliance**: built in one place, hauled as a closed object, set by licensed people, started by licensed people, and taken back by licensed people. The homeowner owns the appliance. They do not become a plant operator.

---

## Why size is the argument

A typical commercial generating station is about **1,000 MW**. That is a million kilowatts, a staff, a perimeter, and a town-scale fight.

The Backyard Generating Station makes **5 to 10 kWe** and about **20 to 40 kW of heat**. That heat number is a large home furnace. After shutdown, leftover heat is a small space heater. Next to the 1,000 MW plant, this unit is about **1/100,000 to 1/200,000** the electrical size. Chapter 5 is the vault at that size. Chapter 9 is the fuel physics that makes leftover heat a space heater and a runaway a small energy. The approval claim follows from it:

- The object is too small for leftover heat to run away into the neighborhood.
- It ships and sits as one sealed vault. Nobody on the lot opens it.
- The lawn target is a few mrem a year extra, if anything, including with the lid bare (Chapter 3).
- Install looks like a buried tank plus a solar interconnect (Chapter 4).

A rulebook written for a commercial plant will block a house product even when the physics is friendly. The project’s plan is a **size-based approval or exemption**: one certified design, made in a factory, installed many times, rather than a custom power-plant license on every lot.

That approval does not exist yet.

---

## Permits towns already know how to issue

Towns already permit:

- a buried concrete tank (septic, propane vault)
- a solar inverter, a battery, and a two-way meter
- a boiler-room heat exchanger
- heavy freight and a crane in a driveway

The federal piece that does not yet exist is permission for the sealed core inside the vault to be *that* small, *that* closed, and *that* numerous. The closest existing ideas are sealed sources and factory-built packages that move as a unit and come back as a unit. The Backyard Generating Station is asking to be closer to those than to a site-built plant.

The homeowner’s permits stay local and familiar: dig, set, interconnect, plumb. How far the **10 by 10 foot** patch sits from the house is that local buried-tank conversation. The core never becomes a backyard license.

---

## Who does what

| Who | What they do | What they do not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Factory | Builds, fuels in a shutdown state, seals, ships one design | Does not open it on the lot |
| Licensed hauler | Moves a closed, locked vault | Does not start it |
| Licensed set crew | Digs, cranes, chase, backfill | Does not run the core |
| Licensed startup | Releases shutdown locks after burial and inspection | Does not hand the owner a key to those locks |
| Electrician / plumber | Utility room: Stirling, inverter, gateway, battery, heat loop | Do not enter the vault |
| Utility | Interconnect and sell-back rules | Does not own the vault |
| Homeowner | Thermostat, bill, optional status screen | Does not operate, refuel, or open |
| Licensed take-back (Chapter 7) | Locks in, crane out, process | Does not leave parts in the shed |

If any of those rows becomes a homeowner weekend job, the product is wrong. The core is never a backyard task.

## What the exemption is asking

In plain language, the paper would say:

1. This is one manufactured design, not a custom plant.
2. Thermal power is furnace-scale (tens of kW, not thousands of MW), about 1/100,000 the electrical size of a 1,000 MW plant.
3. The vault is sealed at the factory and opened only at a licensed facility at the end.
4. Transport, set, startup, and take-back are licensed steps.
5. Public dose at the grass is designed to a few mrem a year, with the vault alone as the shield.
6. The owner cannot start, stop the core, or move fuel.
7. A failed unit is a crane event, not a local teardown.

Call that an exemption, a general license, or a product certificate. The name matters less than the shape: **approve the object once, install it many times**, the way a listed inverter is listed once and hung in a thousand garages.

Until that exists, every early unit is a special case and will cost like one. Chapter 2’s early-production band is that fact in dollars.

---

## What sits next to the stamp

**Utility interconnection** is a separate yes. A house that can export 35,000 to 59,000 kWh a year will hit sell-back caps and rate design. That is a price-and-tariff issue. It is separate from approval of the core. Chapter 2 already says payback depends on what the utility pays for extra power.

**A federal credit like solar’s** is a separate yes. Solar’s early years used a large investment tax credit. The public case here is carbon-free power at the house, fewer 1,000 MW plants towns refuse, and generation that still works at night. It is a policy path, not a promise.

**Insurance, appraisal, and resale** will lag, the way they lagged for rooftop solar. Until this is common, a buried vault plus a utility room will be a new line on a homeowners policy and a new note on an appraisal.

**Town politics** will take time. Chapter 3’s playset number has to be true and measurable. If the yard needed a keep-off fence, the vault would have failed its own dose target.

---

## What this chapter covered

This is the approval chapter: size as the argument, a factory-sealed appliance rather than a commercial plant, licensed hands at every step that touches the core, and a first stamp that does not exist yet. Chapter 5 is why the size claim is honest. Chapter 9 is the fuel and the shutdown physics behind that claim. Chapter 10 is what still has to be shown, including which federal rules exist (plant licenses, Type B transport, public dose) and which house-appliance stamp does not. Chapter 7 is the take-back that makes “never opened on the lot” true at the end. The paperwork should look like approving a manufactured appliance, not siting a power plant.
