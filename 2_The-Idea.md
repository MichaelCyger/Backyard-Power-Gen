# Backyard Generating Station

## The idea

A house should be able to make its own power, keep the lights on in a storm, heat the rooms it already knows how to heat, and sell what it does not use. It should do that without covering the roof in glass, without a 25-year bet on a leaky flashing, and without a fuel truck in the driveway.

That is the Backyard Generating Station: a sealed, factory-built appliance, about the size of a large septic tank, set five to ten feet down in the yard. A licensed crew installs it once. Nobody on the property ever opens it. It makes electricity for the house, stores the extra in a wall battery, sends overflow to the grid, and offers leftover heat to the heating system many newer American homes already have.

It is meant to be compared, at the same address, to rooftop solar: what you pay, what you maintain, what you get, and what you can sell back.

---

## What it is

The station is a lead-lined concrete vault, about the size and weight class of the buried tanks already common in American yards (a septic tank or a buried propane tank). A small excavator digs the hole. A crane sets the vault. The crew buries it. It is never serviced in place. It is designed to run for on the order of a century and then be lifted out as one piece.

Above ground you see almost nothing. The house sees four familiar things:

- a circuit that feeds the electrical panel, the same way a backup generator or a solar system does
- a wall battery that stores extra electricity so the house can use more than the station is making at that exact moment (oven, dryer, and air conditioner at once)
- an inverter, the same kind of box a solar install uses, that turns the station’s output into ordinary house current and talks to the utility
- a closed water loop, where the house can use it, that hands leftover heat to systems that already run on hot water

The homeowner does not operate a plant. They own an appliance.

---

## How big this is

Power is easier to feel with a few anchors.

A typical American house uses about 10,000 to 12,000 kWh in a year (kilowatt-hours: how much energy you used *over time*). That is, on average, a little over 1 kW (kilowatts: how hard power is flowing *right now*), all day, every day, and can spike to 10 or 20 kW when the air conditioner, range, and dryer hit at once.

Some houses use much more. A high-use house can run **$400 to $600 a month** for electricity (**about $5,000 to $7,000 a year**). That is more kilowatt-hours, a higher price per kWh, or both. At ordinary rates that is on the order of 20,000 to 35,000 kWh a year, an average of about 2.5 to 4 kW, with the same kind of evening spikes. The Backyard Generating Station is sized for that high end as well as for the typical house.

Rooftop solar on that same house is usually an 8 to 12 kW array. On a good sun day it can outrun the house. At night, and through a week of coastal weather, it makes nothing. That is why solar quotes always include the roof, the weather, and a battery if you want the house to keep working after dark.

A utility-scale solar farm is the same idea, multiplied: tens or hundreds of megawatts (1 MW = 1,000 kW), spread over fields, feeding the grid rather than one kitchen.

A typical commercial generating station of the large kind most people picture is about 1,000 MW. That is a million kilowatts, enough, in round numbers, for several hundred thousand homes. It is a campus: cooling towers, switchyards, a security perimeter, a staff.

The Backyard Generating Station sits at the *house* end of that range, not the campus end. It is sized for **5 to 10 kWe** (kilowatts of electricity: the “e” just means this number is electrical output, not heat) of steady output. That is in the same neighborhood as a rooftop solar array, except it does not care whether the sun is out.

It does not try to match the 20 kW spike. The battery does that. The station fills the battery the way a well fills a cistern. When the cistern is full and the house is satisfied, the extra goes to the grid.

**A note on the units.** kW is the size of the pipe. kWh is the water that went through it. If the station makes 8 kW for one hour, that is 8 kWh, about what a central air conditioner uses in an hour, or a little less than a day’s worth of a modest house that is not heating or cooling hard. Over a year, 8 kW running most of the time is on the order of 70,000 kWh. A typical house that uses 12,000 kWh has a lot left to sell. A high-use house that uses 20,000 to 35,000 kWh still has tens of thousands of kWh left to sell, or to put into a second car, a workshop, or a storm reserve.

| What | Typical size | What that means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| House, typical average | ~1 kW | About 24 kWh/day, or 10,000 to 12,000 kWh/year. About $2,000 a year at 18¢/kWh. |
| House, high-use average | ~2.5 to 4 kW | About 20,000 to 35,000 kWh/year. About $5,000 to $7,000 a year ($400 to $600 a month). |
| House, peak | 10 to 20 kW | Air conditioner, range, and dryer at once. The battery covers this. |
| Rooftop solar | 8 to 12 kW | Same house-scale neighborhood. Makes power only when the sun is out. |
| Backyard Generating Station | 5 to 10 kWe | Same neighborhood as rooftop solar, as a steady output, sun or not. |
| Utility solar farm | tens to hundreds of MW | Fields. Feeds the grid, not one kitchen. (1 MW = 1,000 kW) |
| Large commercial station | ~1,000 MW | A campus. On the order of several hundred thousand homes. |

You do not need to build a 1,000 MW commercial station to serve those several hundred thousand households. You can build one right-sized unit per house and serve the need *where it already is*, the same placement idea as a rooftop solar array, without the campus, the cooling towers, or the long wires in between. The array is the proof that house-scale generation is a normal way to site power. The station is the version that still works when the sun does not.

## A day in the life

The station runs as a quiet, steady source. The battery covers the short spikes.

Morning: the house wakes up. The battery covers the coffee-and-shower bump. The station is already making power, so the battery does not drain far.

Afternoon: the house is light. The battery fills. If it is full, the inverter exports to the meter the same way a solar system does.

Evening: oven, laundry, screens. The battery covers the peak. The station keeps topping it off. On the off chance the house wants more than the battery still holds (a rare, hard spike), the panel just pulls the rest from the grid, the same way it does today. The lights do not go out. You buy a bit of power for an hour instead of a whole day’s worth.

Night: same as afternoon, without the sun argument. This is the like-for-like point versus solar. The comparison is not which one works at noon. It is annual kilowatt-hours at *this* house, plus what still works when the weather does not.

Heat is a second product of the same day, not leftover scrap. The station makes electricity and warmth together. In winter that warmth heats the house. In summer the house may only want showers, or it can send the rest to a greenhouse. Later chapters treat the hardware. The idea is simple: use the warmth where a pipe can take it.

---

## Heat the house already knows how to use

A lot of newer houses in the United States already heat with hot water in pipes, in the floor, in a tank, or in a coil, not only with hot air blown through vents.

The station never mixes its insides with the house. Heat crosses through a sealed metal exchanger: two water loops share warmth through a wall, and the waters never touch. A home boiler already works this way. What the house already has can take that water:

- **Radiant floors.** Plastic heating pipe (PEX) in a slab or under a floor typically wants water in a modest range, roughly 80 to 120°F. That is the temperature leftover warmth can supply. It is not furnace-flame temperature.
- **Indirect tanks and buffer tanks.** Many boiler rooms already have a tank with a coil in it. The station can be the thing that keeps that tank hot, for showers and laundry, and as stored heat for the heating loop.
- **Hydro-air.** Some houses have both a boiler and ducts. A coil of hot-water pipe sits in the air handler (the big box that already blows air through the house). The station heats that coil. The thermostat and the blower stay the same.
- **A high-efficiency boiler loop.** Newer houses with a condensing boiler already have a hot-water piping loop. The station ties in as a second heat source, isolated, and the boiler becomes backup or peak.
- **Air-to-water heat pumps.** A growing share of new work uses a heat pump to warm a tank of water. The station can preheat that tank so the heat pump does less work, or carry the load when that is the better choice.
- **A greenhouse.** If the house has no hot-water heating loop, or if it does not want the warmth in summer, the same loop can run to a greenhouse. Year-round vegetables and fruit become possible in climates and geographies that would not support that on sunlight and a furnace bill alone. The house still gets the electricity. The plants get the heat.

Houses that are all electric baseboard, or mini-splits with no water loop, still get electricity, battery, and grid sale. They can still take a standalone tank for showers and laundry. They do not get whole-house space heat unless someone adds hot-water piping, or they put the warmth into a greenhouse instead. If the house already has a boiler room or a radiant-floor manifold, the station plugs into hardware the house already speaks. If it does not, you are buying a power appliance. Space heat is then a choice (add piping, add a greenhouse, or use the electricity only).

Winter heating for a typical house is often in the 5 to 15 kW range. A station making 5 to 10 kW of electricity will also be making a couple of times that in heat. That is enough, on many days, to run the floors and the water heater without lighting the old boiler.

## Living with it

Day to day there is nothing to do. The station keeps making power. The battery tops itself off. Extra goes to the grid. Heat follows the thermostat the house already has. When you leave, the setback you already use is enough.

An app is not required for any of that. If there is a phone screen, it is a window, the same kind solar owners already glance at: what the station made, what the house used, what was sold, what is in the battery. Not a set of modes. Not a control room.

The one optional exception is a storm reserve: hold the battery full and stop exporting for a night or two of weather. That can be a single toggle, or automatic from a forecast, the way some battery systems already work. It is a battery setting, not a way of running the station.

## How it makes power

The vault holds a very small nuclear core. Heat comes from splitting atoms in a sealed fuel, at a scale closer to a large home furnace than to a commercial station.

That heat does two jobs for the house at the same time. It makes electricity for the panel. It can also warm rooms, showers, or a greenhouse. Using both is the design. If the house or a greenhouse uses the warmth, most of what the core makes is used, not thrown away.

The numbers stay small on purpose.

A house-scale electrical output of 5 to 10 kW goes with about **20 to 40 kW of heat** from the core. That is furnace scale, not power-plant scale. After the unit is shut down, the leftover heat that has to go somewhere drops to about as much as a small space heater, then lower over the following hours and days. Dirt, concrete, and time can take that. There is no cooling tower.

Fuel use at this output, over a century, is measured in kilograms, not tons: about 30 kilograms loaded (about 66 pounds), a paint-can of heavy metal inside a beer-keg-to-dishwasher-sized core, itself inside the septic-tank vault. Chapter 8 is the arithmetic. The vault is the thing the crane sees. The core is never a backyard project.

Because the core is run cold and slow (a trickle of heat in a lot of mass), it can be built for a very long sealed life. Material mixed into the fuel is used up as the fuel is used up, so the output stays even. There is no refueling truck. There is no hatch for the owner.

If the vault ever gets far hotter than it should, a passive layer above the core is meant to melt, flow, and set into a glass-like mass that stills the reaction and locks the insides in place. The whole vault stays a single object. That is a last-ditch behavior. It is not something expected to happen in the life of the unit. The ordinary safety case is simpler: the unit is too small for leftover heat to run away, it sits in earth, and it does not rely on a pump staying on.

### From the factory to first power

The vault is not “on” when it is built, and it is not on when it rides on a truck.

Fresh fuel has never been used, so it is not already hot. In the factory the core is assembled in a shutdown state: locks and control elements are in the position that keeps it from making heat. The vault is sealed around that. What leaves the plant is a heavy, closed object that is not producing electricity and not producing heat.

It is trucked like other permitted heavy freight and set with a crane. The crew connects the house wires, the inverter, the battery, and, if the house will use it, the water loop. Inspectors sign off.

Only after it is buried and connected does a licensed startup happen. The shutdown locks are released in a controlled procedure. That is the first time the core makes heat. Until that step, there is nothing to cool and nothing to shield a neighbor from in the “running plant” sense. At the end of life the same locks go back in before the crane returns.

This is also why a size-based path is part of the idea. A 1,000 MW station is a power plant. A 30 kW heat source in a factory-sealed cask is an appliance. The project’s regulatory aim is not to site a campus behind a house. It is to get a manufactured, never-opened unit approved, or exempted, because it is this small, installed by licensed people, and left closed until a licensed crew takes it away.

## What it costs

These are high-level bands for a production unit, not a first prototype. Early units cost more. The check you write is in today’s dollars. The bill you stop paying is not frozen. The country is heading into a stretch where most regions will be short of power, and the cents you pay per kWh have been rising and are expected to keep rising. That is part of cost, not a footnote after payback.

**The station itself.** The sealed vault is the manufactured product. In volume, think in the same world as a serious whole-home energy project: tens of thousands of dollars, not hundreds of thousands, once it is a factory object rather than a custom machine. The first ones will not be that.

**Setting it.** A contractor digs a hole five to ten feet down, a crane sets the vault, and the crew beds and buries it. The vault has to be able to sit wet, because a lot of yards, including waterfront ones, will flood in the life of the unit. In ordinary soil that buried-tank job is often **$10,000 to $20,000**. If the crew hits rock, or the hole fills with groundwater, the same work can run **$25,000 to $40,000**.

**Power tie-in.** Panel work, the inverter, and the utility hookup: the same class of electrical work as a solar install. Roughly **$8,000 to $15,000**, depending on the service and the utility.

**Battery.** Two or three wall units in the Tesla-class size (about 13 kWh each) to cover peaks and a storm night: often **$20,000 to $40,000** installed. This is the same kind of line item solar already has if you want the house to work after dark.

**Heat tie-in.** If the house already has a radiant-floor manifold, a hot-water tank, or a boiler loop, an isolated exchanger and controls are a few thousand dollars to about **$10,000**. If the house has no hot-water heating pipes, you either skip space heat, add a tank and piping on purpose, or run a loop to a greenhouse.

**All-in at a house that can use the heat:**

- **Early production:** **$90,000 to $160,000** is the honest band while the vault is still a new manufactured object.
- **Factory scale** (the vault is an ordinary appliance, built in numbers): a working target is **$55,000 to $95,000** all-in, because the vault gets cheaper and the hole, the inverter, and the batteries do not vanish.
- **With a government credit like the one solar received** (a federal investment tax credit, often 30% in solar’s early years, applied to the generating equipment): early production could net in a **$70,000 to $120,000** band, and factory scale in a **$40,000 to $70,000** band. That credit is a policy path, not a promise. Solar got one because the country wanted more carbon-free generation at the house. The same argument applies here, with a second one: every house-scale station is a house that does not need a new 1,000 MW plant sited in someone else’s town. Communities already refuse those plants. The United States already generates almost all of the electricity it uses; the stronger public case is carbon-free power at the load, fewer giant stations, and a factory product that does not depend on a roof full of imported panels.

### What a house uses, and how long the check takes to come back

Household electricity is a range, not one number. Some months are higher (air conditioning, electric heat) and some are lower. The yearly total is what payback uses.

- **Typical house:** about **10,000 to 12,000 kWh a year** (about 900 kWh a month). At a recent US average of about **18 cents per kWh**, that is about **$2,000 a year**, or about $160 to $170 a month. Space heat and hot water are often a second bill of **$800 to $2,000 a year** if they are not already on that electric meter.
- **High-use house:** about **$400 to $600 a month**, or **$5,000 to $7,000 a year**. A $560 month is about $6,700 a year. That may be 20,000 to 35,000 kWh (a bigger house, more electric loads), a higher cents-per-kWh rate, or both. On a bill this large, electric heat or a heat pump is often already inside the $560. Do not add a second heat line on top unless the house still pays a separate gas or oil bill.

The Backyard Generating Station is sized at 5 to 10 kWe of steady output. At 8 kWe, running most of the year:

```
8 kW × 8,760 hours in a year ≈ 70,000 kWh
```

A typical house keeps about 11,000 kWh of that and can sell about **59,000 kWh**. A high-use house keeps about 20,000 to 35,000 kWh and can still sell **35,000 to 50,000 kWh**. Either way, 8 to 10 kWe covers the high end; 5 kWe is a tighter fit if the house averages near 4 kW.

Rooftop solar at the same house is usually an 8 to 12 kW array. It makes about **10,000 to 14,000 kWh a year** on a decent roof, and less on a shaded, cloudy, or low-sun roof. That can match a typical house and not much leftover. On a high-use house it covers only part of the bill. It does not make space heat unless the house already heats with electricity.

So the two systems are not the same size in *delivered* energy. Solar is built to match a typical house. The Backyard Generating Station, if it runs as a steady 8 kW, makes several times even a high-use house’s electric load. Payback then depends on two prices: what you no longer pay the utility (and any leftover heating fuel), and what the utility pays you for the extra.

Simple payback is cost divided by a year’s savings **at today’s rate**, as if the bill never moved. That is the snapshot in the tables below. It overstates the years if rates keep climbing, which they have been. These are round numbers for a factory-scale Backyard Generating Station after a solar-style 30% credit (**$40,000 to $70,000**), next to a complete solar-plus-battery job at the same meter.

**Typical house (~$2,000 electric bill):**

| | What you pay (net) | Electricity made in a year | A year’s savings (round) | Simple payback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solar + battery, ordinary roof | $35,000 to $55,000 | 10,000 to 14,000 kWh (about what the house uses) | About $2,000 (the electric bill) | **12 to 20 years** |
| Solar + battery, shaded, cloudy, or low-sun roof | $35,000 to $55,000, sometimes more | Less than the house uses | About $1,200 to $1,800 | **25 to 30 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid near the retail rate (~18¢/kWh) | $40,000 to $70,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $12,000 to $14,000 (bill + heat + sell-back) | **3 to 6 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid at a lower wholesale-style rate (~5¢/kWh) | $40,000 to $70,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $5,000 to $7,000 | **7 to 12 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid nothing (house offset and heat only) | $40,000 to $70,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $2,800 to $4,000 | **13 to 23 years** |

**High-use house (~$5,000 to $7,000 electric bill, about $400 to $600 a month):**

| | What you pay (net) | Electricity made in a year | A year’s savings (round) | Simple payback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solar + battery, ordinary roof | $35,000 to $55,000 | 10,000 to 14,000 kWh (only part of the bill) | About $2,000 to $3,000 | **15 to 25 years** |
| Solar + battery, shaded, cloudy, or low-sun roof | $35,000 to $55,000, sometimes more | Less than that | About $1,200 to $2,000 | **25 to 40 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid near the retail rate | $40,000 to $70,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $12,000 to $16,000 (the $5,000 to $7,000 bill plus sell-back) | **3 to 6 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid at a lower wholesale-style rate (~5¢/kWh) | $40,000 to $70,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $8,000 to $10,000 | **4 to 9 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid nothing (house offset only) | $40,000 to $70,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $5,000 to $7,000 | **6 to 14 years** |

Solar’s problem on a high-use house is simple: the array still makes about 10,000 to 14,000 kWh, so most of a $6,700 bill remains. The Backyard Generating Station’s problem is still the sell-back price, not whether it can cover the house.

### The grid is getting short, and the rate is going up

For about twenty years, US electricity use was almost flat. That stretch is over. Data centers, factories, electric cars, and houses switching from gas to electric heat are adding load faster than new plants and transmission are getting built. Demand is on track to be on the order of **20% higher by 2030** and **about 40% higher by the mid-2030s**. The spare generating capacity sitting above what the grid already needs is already thin (a few percent nationally). Some of the biggest regional grids already have little or no extra room. New 1,000 MW stations take years and are the plants communities already refuse. So for the next decade to a few decades, most places in the country are more likely to be short of power than swimming in it.

When the grid is short, two things happen to a household bill.

**The cents per kWh go up.** They already have. The US residential average has climbed from the low teens (cents per kWh) a decade ago to about **18 cents** now, and recent years have been on the order of **4% to 6% a year**. Forecasts for the rest of this decade often land in a **15% to 40% higher bill by 2030**, depending on the utility: more plants, more wires, and more competition from big new loads. A $2,000 typical bill becomes about $2,300 to $2,800. A $6,700 high-use bill becomes about $7,700 to $9,400. After that, the climb does not automatically stop.

**The kilowatt-hour you do not buy is worth more each year.** Simple payback pretends year 10 is still $2,000 or $6,700. It is not. At 5% a year, a $6,700 bill is about **$10,900 in ten years**. Every year you are not paying it, the savings get larger, so the remaining balance on the $40,000 to $70,000 check shrinks faster than the frozen-rate tables show.

A compact way to see it: same $40,000 to $70,000 Backyard Generating Station, same solar jobs, if the rate rises about **5% a year** (in the middle of the recent pace):

| | Frozen-rate payback (tables above) | If rates rise ~5%/year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar + battery, typical house, ordinary roof | 12 to 20 years | **10 to 16 years** |
| Solar + battery, typical house, weak roof | 25 to 30 years | **18 to 24 years** |
| Solar + battery, high-use house, ordinary roof | 15 to 25 years | **12 to 20 years** |
| Solar + battery, high-use house, weak roof | 25 to 40 years | **18 to 30 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, typical house, extra paid ~5¢ | 7 to 12 years | **6 to 10 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, typical house, extra paid nothing | 13 to 23 years | **10 to 15 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, high-use house, extra paid ~5¢ | 4 to 9 years | **4 to 7 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, high-use house, extra paid nothing | 6 to 14 years | **5 to 10 years** |

Retail-rate sell-back rows were already a handful of years. Rising rates shave those too, but the bigger move is in the rows that used to look slow. A typical house with no sell-back stops looking like a 13-to-23-year solar cousin and starts looking like **10 to 15 years**. A high-use house with no sell-back lands around **5 to 10 years** on the bill alone.

Solar also gets a shorter payback when rates rise. It only gets that help on the 10,000 to 14,000 kWh it actually makes. The Backyard Generating Station gets it on the whole house bill (and on whatever extra the utility still pays for). On a high-use house, that is the difference between offsetting a slice of a rising bill and stepping off the bill.

A short grid also changes what extra power is worth. If the country is short, a house that can sell 35,000 to 59,000 kWh is offering something the system needs. That does not guarantee retail-rate net metering. It does make the “utility pays nothing” row less likely to stay true for decades, and it makes wholesale-style rates more likely to rise along with retail. Later chapters can put a real tariff on that. The high-level point is: the frozen-rate tables are the conservative picture.

The other half of a short grid is not a dollar on a spreadsheet. It is whether the house still has power when the region does not have enough. A Backyard Generating Station plus a battery is generation at the load. It does not wait on a plant that has not been sited yet.

The honest fork is still the sell-back price, plus how large the house is, plus how fast the rate climbs. Batteries wear out and get replaced; that is true for solar-plus-battery as well, and it is not in the divisions above. Later chapters can put a real tariff and a real heat load on one address. The claim here is that at $40,000 to $70,000, against a range from a ~$2,000 typical bill to a ~$5,000 to $7,000 high-use bill, in a country that is getting short of plants and is already raising rates, the Backyard Generating Station’s payback gets better with time, and solar’s payback is still capped by how much of that rising bill the roof can actually cover.

Maintenance, for the owner, is the battery and the inverter: the same wear items solar already has. The buried vault is not a filter, a roof, or an oil change.

---

## When it is done

Nothing is opened in the yard.

At the end of life, or if a unit is swapped early, a licensed crew isolates the house, cranes the vault out as one piece, and sets the next one in the same hole. The old vault goes to a licensed facility. Shielding, metal, and concrete are recycled or reused where that is possible. The homeowner does not store, cut, or dispose of anything.

The fuel that did the work began as about 30 kilograms (66 pounds) of heavy metal. After a century, what cannot be recycled or reused is still in that size class: a paint-can receptacle at a licensed site, not a hillside of waste behind the house. The septic-tank vault was almost all shield and box. That is what the crane was for.

A replacement price should be sold as a **swap**: new unit, removal, haul, and processing of the old one, plus the reconnect. It should not be a surprise line item after someone has lived with the appliance for decades. Whether that is a prepaid end-of-life fee, an included core-exchange, or a posted swap tariff is a later-chapter problem. The rule for this chapter is: replacement means the old station leaves, and the cost of that leaving is part of replacement, not a hidden chore for the household.

## Benefits

- **Power that does not care about the roof or the weather.** Nights, storms, and long gray stretches are ordinary, not a failure mode.
- **A like-for-like bill versus solar.** Same house, same meter, same battery habit, plus heat if the house can take it, compared on delivered energy and sell-back, not on panel count.
- **Heat the house can already use, or a greenhouse if it cannot.** Floors, tanks, air-handler coils, boiler loops, or year-round growing.
- **Sell-back when the battery is full.** The electrical path is the one solar already uses.
- **No holes in the roof.** No array in a wind corridor. No 25-year flashing bet.
- **No fuel deliveries for the life of the unit.**
- **No day-to-day operation.** The thermostat and the utility meter are enough. An app, if you want one, is a status screen.
- **An install towns already know how to permit.** A buried concrete vault, a crane, backfill.
- **A size that is meant to be regulated as an appliance.** The whole point of staying at house scale is a path that does not look like siting a 1,000 MW campus.
- **A path that can sit next to the solar tax credit.** Carbon-free generation at the house, and fewer new commercial stations that communities do not want nearby.
- **Power at the house while the country is getting short.** Demand is rising faster than plants are being built. Rates have been climbing about 4% to 6% a year. A unit that covers the whole bill, not a slice of it, gets more valuable as that happens.
- **A clean end.** One lift, one replacement, processing included in the swap. Recycle and reuse where possible. What cannot be processed fits a paint-can receptacle after a 100-year life.

## Detriments

- **Early units will be expensive.** A complete solar-plus-battery job at the same house is the fair price comparison, not a panel-only ad. Solar’s first years were also expensive, and a federal credit (often 30%) brought the net cost down. The same kind of credit could apply here. Until it does, and until the vault is a factory object, the check is large.
- **It is not a Saturday project.** It needs permits, a licensed set, a utility hookup, and extra piping if you want the heat in the house or in a greenhouse.
- **Not every house can use the heat indoors without added hardware.** Mini-splits and electric baseboard still get electricity. Whole-house space heat needs hot-water pipes, or the warmth goes to a greenhouse, showers, or a dedicated cooler.
- **In summer the house often does not want the warmth.** The station is still making heat while it makes electricity. If that heat is not sent to showers or a greenhouse, it has to be dumped on purpose through a small outdoor radiator (the same kind of box a heat pump already uses on the side of a house). If it is not dumped on purpose, it soaks into the soil around the vault. That does not heat the neighborhood, but that patch of yard runs warmer than the rest of the lot, and a vault close to the house can make the air conditioner work a little harder. August heat is a design job, not a free extra.
- **The first approval does not exist yet.** The exemption-by-size story is the strategy. It is not a stamp on the wall.
- **The buried unit is a controlled object.** It is sealed and heavy on purpose. That is a benefit for the household and a responsibility for the people who make, move, and take it back.
- **End of life is specialized.** The swap includes removal and processing. That is not the same as stacking old panels on a pallet, even if the leftover fuel fits a paint-can receptacle.
- **Insurance, appraisal, and resale will have to learn a new appliance.** Solar already fought this fight. This one is newer and heavier.
- **Public comfort will lag the physics.** A septic-tank-sized vault in the yard will need a clearer story than a datasheet.

## What this document is

This is the idea at house scale, and only house scale: one family, one yard, one meter, compared to solar at that same meter. The later chapters are 3 Yard Safety, 4 The House, 5 The Vault, 6 The Approval, and 7 The Swap. The claim here is only that the physics is in the right size class, the house already has ways to use the heat, the owner’s life can stay simple, and the end of the story is a crane, not a project that never ends.
