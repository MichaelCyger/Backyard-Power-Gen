# Backyard Generating Station

## The idea

A house should be able to make its own power, keep the lights on in a storm, heat the rooms it already knows how to heat, and sell what it does not use. It should do that without covering the roof in solar panels, without depending on roof seals that can leak for 25 years, and without a fuel truck in the driveway.

That is the Backyard Generating Station: a sealed, factory-built appliance, about the size of a large septic tank, with **ten feet of soil above the lid**. American yards already bury tanks this way (septic tanks, underground propane, heating-oil tanks). Ten feet of cover is on the deep side of that habit, closer to a large buried fuel tank than to a shallow septic lid. The object itself needs about a **10 by 10 foot** patch of yard. How far it sits from the house is a local permit question.

A licensed crew installs it once. Nobody on the property ever opens it. It makes electricity for the house, stores the extra in a wall battery, sends overflow to the grid, and offers leftover heat to the heating system many newer American homes already have.

It is meant to be compared, at the same address, to rooftop solar: what you pay, what you maintain, what you get, and what you can sell back.

---

## What it is

The station is a lead-lined concrete vault, about the size and weight class of the buried tanks already common in American yards. A small excavator digs the hole. A crane sets the vault. The crew puts ten feet of soil back on the lid and the grass goes back on top. It is never serviced in place. It is designed to run for on the order of a century and then be lifted out as one piece.

Above ground you see almost nothing. Heat and a few wires reach the house through a **chase**: a buried pipe run from the underside of the vault to a **utility room**. That room can be in the house (a basement, a garage wall) or a small shed next to it. The house sees four familiar things:

- a circuit that feeds the electrical panel, the same way a backup generator or a solar system does
- a wall battery that stores extra electricity so the house can use more than the station is making at that exact moment (oven, dryer, and air conditioner at once)
- an inverter, the same kind of box a solar install uses, that turns the station’s output into ordinary house current and talks to the utility
- a closed water loop, where the house can use it, that hands leftover heat to systems that already run on hot water

The homeowner does not operate a plant. They own an appliance.

## How big this is

Power is easier to feel with a few anchors.

A typical American house uses about 10,000 to 12,000 kWh in a year (kilowatt-hours: how much energy you used *over time*). That is, on average, a little over 1 kW (kilowatts: how hard power is flowing *right now*), all day, every day, and can spike to 10 or 20 kW when the air conditioner, range, and dryer hit at once.

Some houses use much more. A high-use house can run **$400 to $600 a month** for electricity (**about $5,000 to $7,000 a year**). That is more kilowatt-hours, a higher price per kWh, or both. At ordinary rates that is on the order of 20,000 to 35,000 kWh a year, an average of about 2.5 to 4 kW, with the same kind of evening spikes. The Backyard Generating Station is sized for that high end as well as for the typical house.

Rooftop solar on that same house is usually an 8 to 12 kW array. On a good sun day it can outrun the house. At night, and through a week of coastal weather, it makes nothing. That is why solar quotes always include the roof, the weather, and a battery if you want the house to keep working after dark.

A utility-scale solar farm is the same idea, multiplied: tens or hundreds of megawatts (1 MW = 1,000 kW), spread over fields, feeding the grid rather than one kitchen.

A typical commercial generating station of the large kind most people picture is about 1,000 MW. That is a million kilowatts, enough, in round numbers, for several hundred thousand homes. It is a campus: cooling towers, switchyards, a security perimeter, a staff.

The Backyard Generating Station sits at the house end of that range. It is sized for **5 to 10 kWe** (kilowatts of electricity: the “e” just means this number is electrical output, not heat) of steady output. Next to a 1,000 MW plant that is about **1/100,000 to 1/200,000** the electrical size. It is a home-furnace-scale heat source that also makes power. That is why it belongs in a yard the way a buried tank does, and why it is not a commercial plant next door.

It is in the same size class as a rooftop solar array, except it keeps making power when the sun is out and when it is not.

It does not try to match the 20 kW spike. The battery does that. The station keeps charging the battery. When the battery is full and the house has what it needs, extra power goes to the grid.

**A note on the units.** kW is the size of the pipe. kWh is the water that went through it. If the station makes 8 kW for one hour, that is 8 kWh, about what a central air conditioner uses in an hour, or a little less than a day’s worth of a modest house that is not heating or cooling hard. Over a year, 8 kW running most of the time is on the order of 70,000 kWh. A typical house that uses 12,000 kWh has a lot left to sell. A high-use house that uses 20,000 to 35,000 kWh still has tens of thousands of kWh left to sell, or to put into a second car, a workshop, or a storm reserve.

| What | Typical size | What that means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| House, typical average | ~1 kW | About 24 kWh/day, or 10,000 to 12,000 kWh/year. About $2,000 a year at 18¢/kWh. |
| House, high-use average | ~2.5 to 4 kW | About 20,000 to 35,000 kWh/year. About $5,000 to $7,000 a year ($400 to $600 a month). |
| House, peak | 10 to 20 kW | Air conditioner, range, and dryer at once. The battery covers this. |
| Rooftop solar | 8 to 12 kW | Same house-scale neighborhood. Makes power only when the sun is out. |
| Backyard Generating Station | 5 to 10 kWe | Same neighborhood as rooftop solar, as a steady output, sun or not. |
| Utility solar farm | tens to hundreds of MW | Fields. Feeds the grid, not one kitchen. (1 MW = 1,000 kW) |
| Large commercial station | ~1,000 MW | A campus. About 100,000 to 200,000 times the electrical size of this unit. |

You do not need to build a 1,000 MW commercial station to serve those several hundred thousand households. You can build one right-sized unit per house and serve the need where the load already is, the same placement idea as a rooftop solar array, without the campus, the cooling towers, or the long wires in between. Rooftop solar already shows that house-scale generation is a normal way to site power. The station is the version that still works when the sun does not.

## A day in the life

The station runs as a quiet, steady source. The battery covers the short spikes.

Morning: the house wakes up. The battery covers the coffee-and-shower bump. The station is already making power, so the battery does not drain far.

Afternoon: the house is light. The battery fills. If it is full, the inverter exports to the meter the same way a solar system does.

Evening: oven, laundry, screens. The battery covers the peak. The station keeps topping it off. On the off chance the house wants more than the battery still holds (a rare, hard spike), the panel just pulls the rest from the grid, the same way it does today. The lights do not go out. You buy a bit of power for an hour instead of a whole day’s worth.

Night: same as afternoon, except solar is not making power. This is the like-for-like point versus solar. Compare yearly kilowatt-hours at this house, plus what still works when the weather is bad.

Heat is a second product of the same day. The station makes electricity and warmth together. In winter that warmth heats the house. In summer the house may only want showers, or it can send the rest to a greenhouse. Later chapters treat the hardware. The idea is simple: use the warmth where a pipe can take it.

---

## Heat the house already knows how to use

A lot of newer houses in the United States already heat with hot water in pipes, in the floor, in a tank, or in a coil, not only with hot air blown through vents.

The station never mixes its insides with the house. Heat crosses through a sealed metal exchanger: two water loops share warmth through a wall, and the waters never touch. A home boiler already works this way. What the house already has can take that water:

- **Radiant floors.** Plastic heating pipe (PEX) in a slab or under a floor typically wants water in a modest range, roughly 80 to 120°F. That is the temperature leftover warmth can supply. It is not furnace-flame temperature.
- **Indirect tanks and buffer tanks.** Many boiler rooms already have a tank with a coil in it. The station can be the thing that keeps that tank hot, for showers and laundry, and as stored heat for the heating loop.
- **Hydro-air.** Some houses have both a boiler and ducts. A coil of hot-water pipe sits in the air handler (the big box that already blows air through the house). The station heats that coil. The thermostat and the blower stay the same.
- **A high-efficiency boiler loop.** Newer houses with a condensing boiler already have a hot-water piping loop. The station ties in as a second heat source, isolated, and the boiler becomes backup or peak.
- **Air-to-water heat pumps.** A growing share of new work uses a heat pump to warm a tank of water. The station can preheat that tank so the heat pump does less work, or carry the load when that is the better choice.
- **A greenhouse.** If the house has no hot-water heating loop, or if it does not want the warmth in summer, the same loop can run to a greenhouse. Year-round vegetables and fruit become possible in climates and geographies that would not support that on sunlight and a furnace bill alone. The house still gets the electricity. The plants get the heat.

Houses that are all electric baseboard, or mini-splits with no water loop, still get electricity, battery, and grid sale. They can still take a standalone tank for showers and laundry. They do not get whole-house space heat unless someone adds hot-water piping, or they put the warmth into a greenhouse instead. If the house already has a boiler room or a radiant-floor manifold, the station connects to that hardware. If it does not, you are buying a power appliance. Space heat is then a choice: add piping, add a greenhouse, or use the electricity only.

Winter heating for a typical house is often in the 5 to 15 kW range. A station making 5 to 10 kW of electricity will also be making a couple of times that in heat. That is enough, on many days, to run the floors and the water heater without lighting the old boiler.

## Living with it

Day to day there is nothing to do. The station keeps making power. The battery tops itself off. Extra goes to the grid. Heat follows the thermostat the house already has. When you leave, the setback you already use is enough.

An app is not required. The thermostat and the utility meter are enough. If you install one on a phone or a tablet, it is only a status window, the same habit solar owners already have: what the station made, what the house used, what was sold, what is in the battery. It can make those numbers easier to see. It does not run the station. It has no modes and no switches that start, stop, or change the vault.

## How it makes power

The vault holds a very small nuclear core. Heat comes from splitting atoms in a sealed fuel, at a scale closer to a large home furnace than to a commercial station.

That heat does two jobs for the house at the same time. It makes electricity for the panel. It can also warm rooms, showers, or a greenhouse. Using both is the design. If the house or a greenhouse uses the warmth, most of what the core makes is used, not thrown away.

The numbers stay small on purpose.

A house-scale electrical output of 5 to 10 kW goes with about **20 to 40 kW of heat** from the core. That is furnace scale, not power-plant scale. After the unit is shut down, the leftover heat that has to go somewhere drops to about as much as a small space heater, then lower over the following hours and days. Dirt, concrete, and time can take that. There is no cooling tower.

Fuel use at this output, over a century, is measured in kilograms, not tons: about **30 kilograms (about 66 pounds) at first start**, a paint-can of heavy metal inside a beer-keg-to-dishwasher-sized core, itself inside the septic-tank vault. By the end of life, on the order of 15 to 25 kilograms of that heavy metal has been used up. Chapter 8 is the arithmetic. Chapter 9 is the fuel, the graphite, the sodium in the pipes, and the physics of how the reaction is meant to stop. The vault is the thing the crane sees. The core is never a backyard project.

Because the core is run cold and slow (a trickle of heat in a lot of mass), it can be built for a very long sealed life. Material mixed into the fuel is used up as the fuel is used up, so the output stays even. There is no refueling truck. There is no hatch for the owner.

The fuel and the core are built so heat itself slows the reaction: hotter means less splitting, in milliseconds, without a person or a motor. That is the ordinary safety case. Chapter 9 is that Doppler brake, the drums, and the last-ditch glass. A last-ditch layer above the core can melt and set into a glass-like mass if the vault ever got far hotter than it should. That is not expected to run. The design is meant to never get there. The unit is too small for leftover heat to run away, it sits in earth, and it does not rely on a pump staying on.

### From the factory to first power

The vault is not “on” when it is built, and it is not on when it rides on a truck.

Fresh fuel has never been used, so it is not already hot. In the factory the core is assembled in a shutdown state: locks and control elements are in the position that keeps it from making heat. The vault is sealed around that. What leaves the plant is a heavy, closed object that is not producing electricity and not producing heat.

It is trucked like other permitted heavy freight and set with a crane. The crew connects the house wires, the inverter, the battery, and, if the house will use it, the water loop. Inspectors sign off.

Only after it is buried and connected does a licensed startup happen. The shutdown locks are released in a controlled procedure. That is the first time the core makes heat. Until that step the vault is cold. At the end of life the same locks go back in before the crane returns.

This is also why a size-based path is part of the idea. A 1,000 MW station is a power plant. A 20 to 40 kW heat source in a factory-sealed vault is an appliance, about 1/100,000 the electrical size of that plant. The project’s aim is not to site a campus behind a house. It is to get a manufactured, never-opened unit approved, or exempted, because it is this small, installed by licensed people, and left closed until a licensed crew takes it away.

## What it costs

These are high-level bands for a production unit, not a first prototype. Early units cost more. The check you write is in today’s dollars. The bill you stop paying will not stay at today’s rate. The country is heading into a stretch where most regions will be short of power, and the cents you pay per kWh have been rising and are expected to keep rising. That rising rate belongs in the cost picture.

**The station itself.** The sealed vault is the manufactured product. In volume, think in the same world as a serious whole-home energy project: tens of thousands of dollars, not hundreds of thousands, once it is a factory object rather than a custom machine. The first ones will not be that.

**Setting it.** A contractor digs a hole deep enough for the vault plus **ten feet of soil on the lid**, a crane sets the vault, and the crew beds and buries it. The grass footprint is about **10 by 10 feet**. The vault has to be able to sit wet, because a lot of yards will flood in the life of the unit. In ordinary soil that buried-tank job is often **$10,000 to $20,000**. If the crew hits rock, or the hole fills with groundwater, the same work can run **$25,000 to $40,000**.

**Power tie-in.** Panel work, the inverter, and the utility hookup: the same class of electrical work as a solar install. Roughly **$8,000 to $15,000**, depending on the service and the utility.

**Stirling engines.** Five sealed 2 kWe convertors in the utility room (10 kWe installed, 8 kWe used, one spare). They sit next to the inverter, not in the vault. Each unit is about the size of a large fire extinguisher (about 12 inches across and 18 inches tall, about 110 pounds). Five of them, plus the oil pump and the heat exchanger, fit a basement utility room or a small shed. They do not fit in a closet next to the panel.

Today, at low volume, that set is about **$40,000 to $100,000** (about $8,000 to $20,000 per engine). If they become an ordinary factory part, a working target is **$15,000 to $30,000** for the set. That first set is part of the install check. They last about **10 to 15 years** and get swapped in that room. The swap is a later check, like a furnace. It is not in the simple-payback tables below.

**Battery.** Two or three wall units in the Tesla-class size (about 13 kWh each) to cover peaks and a storm night: often **$20,000 to $40,000** installed. This is the same kind of line item solar already has if you want the house to work after dark.

**Heat tie-in.** If the house already has a radiant-floor manifold, a hot-water tank, or a boiler loop, an isolated exchanger and controls are a few thousand dollars to about **$10,000**. If the house has no hot-water heating pipes, you either skip space heat, add a tank and piping on purpose, or run a loop to a greenhouse.

**All-in at a house that can use the heat:**

- **Early production:** **$130,000 to $260,000** is the honest band while the vault is still a new manufactured object and the Stirling set is still a low-volume part (**$40,000 to $100,000** of that band).
- **Factory scale** (the vault is an ordinary appliance, built in numbers): a working target is **$70,000 to $125,000** all-in. The vault gets cheaper. The hole, the Stirling set (**$15,000 to $30,000**), the inverter, and the batteries do not vanish.
- **With a government credit like the one solar received** (a federal investment tax credit, often 30% in solar’s early years, applied to the generating equipment, including the Stirling set): early production could net in a **$90,000 to $180,000** band, and factory scale in a **$50,000 to $90,000** band. That credit is a policy path, not a promise. Solar got one because the country wanted more carbon-free generation at the house. The same argument applies here, with a second one: every house-scale station is a house that does not need a new 1,000 MW plant sited in someone else’s town. Communities already refuse those plants. The United States already generates almost all of the electricity it uses; the stronger public case is carbon-free power at the load, fewer giant stations, and a factory product that does not depend on a roof full of imported panels.

### What a house uses, and how long the check takes to come back

Household electricity is a range, not one number. Some months are higher (air conditioning, electric heat) and some are lower. The yearly total is what payback uses.

- **Typical house:** about **10,000 to 12,000 kWh a year** (about 900 kWh a month). At a recent US average of about **18 cents per kWh**, that is about **$2,000 a year**, or about $160 to $170 a month. Space heat and hot water are often a second bill of **$800 to $2,000 a year** if they are not already on that electric meter.
- **High-use house:** about **$400 to $600 a month**, or **$5,000 to $7,000 a year**. A $560 month is about $6,700 a year. That may be 20,000 to 35,000 kWh (a bigger house, more electric loads), a higher cents-per-kWh rate, or both. On a bill this large, electric heat or a heat pump is often already inside the $560. Do not add a second heat line on top unless the house still pays a separate gas or oil bill.

The Backyard Generating Station is sized at 5 to 10 kWe of steady output. At 8 kWe, running most of the year:

```
8 kW × 8,760 hours in a year ≈ 70,000 kWh
```

A typical house keeps about 11,000 kWh of that and can sell about **59,000 kWh**. A high-use house keeps about 20,000 to 35,000 kWh and can still sell **35,000 to 50,000 kWh**. Either way, 8 to 10 kWe covers the high end; 5 kWe is a tighter fit if the house averages near 4 kW.

Rooftop solar at the same house is usually an 8 to 12 kW array. It makes about **10,000 to 14,000 kWh a year** on a decent roof, and less on a shaded, cloudy, or low-sun roof. That can match a typical house and not much leftover. On a high-use house it covers only part of the bill. It does not make space heat unless the house already heats with electricity.

So the two systems are not the same size in *delivered* energy. Solar is built to match a typical house. The Backyard Generating Station, if it runs as a steady 8 kW, makes several times even a high-use house’s electric load. Payback then depends on two prices: what you no longer pay the utility (and any leftover heating fuel), and what the utility pays you for the extra.

Simple payback is cost divided by a year’s savings **at today’s rate**, as if the bill never moved. That is the snapshot in the tables below. It overstates the years if rates keep climbing, which they have been. These are round numbers for a factory-scale Backyard Generating Station after a solar-style 30% credit (**$50,000 to $90,000**, including the first Stirling set), next to a complete solar-plus-battery job at the same meter.

**Typical house (~$2,000 electric bill):**

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solar + battery, ordinary roof | $35,000 to $55,000 | 10,000 to 14,000 kWh (about what the house uses) | About $2,000 (the electric bill) | **12 to 20 years** |
| Solar + battery, shaded, cloudy, or low-sun roof | $35,000 to $55,000, sometimes more | Less than the house uses | About $1,200 to $1,800 | **25 to 30 years** |

| Backyard Generating Station, extra paid at a lower wholesale-style rate (~5¢/kWh) | $50,000 to $90,000 | ~70,000 kWh | About $5,000 to $7,000 | **7 to 18 years** |

Solar’s problem on a high-use house is simple: the array still makes about 10,000 to 14,000 kWh, so most of a $6,700 bill remains. The Backyard Generating Station’s problem is still the sell-back price, not whether it can cover the house.

### The grid is getting short, and the rate is going up

For about twenty years, US electricity use was almost flat. That stretch is over. Data centers, factories, electric cars, and houses switching from gas to electric heat are adding load faster than new plants and transmission are getting built. Demand is on track to be on the order of **20% higher by 2030** and **about 40% higher by the mid-2030s**. The spare generating capacity sitting above what the grid already needs is already thin (a few percent nationally). Some of the biggest regional grids already have little or no extra room. New 1,000 MW stations take years and are the plants communities already refuse. So for the next decade to a few decades, most places in the country are more likely to be short of power than swimming in it.

When the grid is short, two things happen to a household bill.

**The cents per kWh go up.** They already have. The US residential average has climbed from the low teens (cents per kWh) a decade ago to about **18 cents** now, and recent years have been on the order of **4% to 6% a year**. Forecasts for the rest of this decade often land in a **15% to 40% higher bill by 2030**, depending on the utility: more plants, more wires, and more competition from big new loads. A $2,000 typical bill becomes about $2,300 to $2,800. A $6,700 high-use bill becomes about $7,700 to $9,400. After that, the climb does not automatically stop.

**The kilowatt-hour you do not buy is worth more each year.** Simple payback pretends year 10 is still $2,000 or $6,700. It is not. At 5% a year, a $6,700 bill is about **$10,900 in ten years**. Every year you are not paying it, the savings get larger, so the remaining balance on the $50,000 to $90,000 check shrinks faster than the frozen-rate tables show.

A compact way to see it: same $50,000 to $90,000 Backyard Generating Station (factory scale, after credit, first Stirling set included), same solar jobs, if the rate rises about **5% a year** (in the middle of the recent pace):

| | Frozen-rate payback (tables above) | If rates rise ~5%/year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar + battery, typical house, ordinary roof | 12 to 20 years | **10 to 16 years** |
| Solar + battery, typical house, weak roof | 25 to 30 years | **18 to 24 years** |
| Solar + battery, high-use house, ordinary roof | 15 to 25 years | **12 to 20 years** |
| Solar + battery, high-use house, weak roof | 25 to 40 years | **18 to 30 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, typical house, extra paid ~5¢ | 7 to 18 years | **6 to 13 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, typical house, extra paid nothing | 13 to 32 years | **10 to 20 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, high-use house, extra paid ~5¢ | 5 to 11 years | **4 to 9 years** |
| Backyard Generating Station, high-use house, extra paid nothing | 7 to 18 years | **6 to 13 years** |

Retail-rate sell-back rows are still a handful of years (**4 to 8** typical, **3 to 8** high-use). Rising rates shave those too. The bigger move is in the rows that look slow at a frozen rate. A typical house with no sell-back is **13 to 32 years** if the rate never moves, and about **10 to 20 years** if it rises 5% a year. A high-use house with no sell-back is **7 to 18 years** frozen, and about **6 to 13 years** if rates rise.

Solar also gets a shorter payback when rates rise. It only gets that help on the 10,000 to 14,000 kWh it actually makes. The Backyard Generating Station gets it on the whole house bill, and on whatever extra the utility still pays for. On a high-use house, solar still covers only part of a rising bill. The station can cover the whole bill.

A short grid also changes what extra power is worth. If the country is short, a house that can sell 35,000 to 59,000 kWh is offering power the system needs. That does not guarantee the utility will pay the full retail rate. It does make the “utility pays nothing” row less likely to stay true for decades, and it makes wholesale-style rates more likely to rise along with retail. Later chapters can put a real tariff on that. The frozen-rate tables are the conservative picture.

A short grid also means the house may still have power when the region does not have enough. A Backyard Generating Station plus a battery makes power at the house. It does not wait on a plant that has not been built yet.

What still decides the years is three ordinary things: what the utility pays you for extra power, how large the house bill is, and how fast that rate climbs. Batteries wear out and get replaced. The Stirling set does too, on a 10 to 15 year clock. That is true for solar-plus-battery as well (the battery), and those later swaps are not in the tables above. The first Stirling set is in the $50,000 to $90,000 install number. Later chapters can put a real utility price and a real heat load on one address. At $50,000 to $90,000, against a range from a ~$2,000 typical bill to a ~$5,000 to $7,000 high-use bill, in a country that is getting short of plants and is already raising rates, the Backyard Generating Station’s payback gets better with time. Solar’s payback is still limited by how much of that rising bill the roof can actually cover.

Owner maintenance is in the **utility room**, not in the hole. The battery, the inverter, the Stirling set, and a pump wear on a decade scale and get swapped in that room. The buried vault is never opened or serviced in the yard. There is no filter to change on it, no roof work, and no oil change.

## When it is done

Nothing is opened in the yard.

At the end of life, or if a unit is swapped early, a licensed crew cranes the vault out as one piece and sets the next one in the same hole. Digging can take a day or two. During that work an existing house stays on the utility, the same way it did before the station was there. The homeowner is without the station (and, if the crew has to open the panel, without house power) only for the **hours** it takes to lock the old vault, lift it, set the new one, and land the pipes and wires again. The battery can cover part of that window. A first install on a new house can take days. That is a construction week. The house can stay on the utility while the crew works. The old vault goes to a licensed facility. Shielding, metal, and concrete are recycled or reused where that is possible. The homeowner does not store, cut, or dispose of anything.

The fuel that did the work began as about 30 kilograms (66 pounds) of heavy metal. After a century, what cannot be recycled or reused is still in that size class: a paint-can receptacle at a licensed site. It does not stay on the property. The septic-tank vault was almost all shield and box. That is what the crane was for.

A replacement price should be sold as a **swap**: new unit, removal, haul, and processing of the old one, plus the reconnect. It should not be a surprise line item after someone has lived with the appliance for decades. Whether that is a prepaid end-of-life fee, an included core-exchange, or a posted swap tariff is a later-chapter question. For this chapter: replacement means the old station leaves, and the cost of that leaving is part of the replacement price. The household does not dispose of the vault.

---

## Benefits

- **Power that does not depend on the roof or the weather.** Nights, storms, and long gray stretches are ordinary running conditions.
- **A like-for-like bill versus solar.** Same house, same meter, same battery habit, plus heat if the house can take it, compared on delivered energy and sell-back, not on panel count.
- **Heat the house can already use, or a greenhouse if it cannot.** Floors, tanks, air-handler coils, boiler loops, or year-round growing.
- **Sell-back when the battery is full.** The electrical path is the one solar already uses.
- **No holes in the roof.** No array in a wind corridor. No 25-year dependence on roof seals.
- **No fuel deliveries for the life of the unit.**
- **No day-to-day operation.** The thermostat and the utility meter are enough. An app, if you want one, is a status screen on a phone or tablet. It does not control the vault.
- **An install towns already know how to permit.** A buried concrete vault, a crane, backfill.
- **A size that is meant to be regulated as an appliance.** The whole point of staying at house scale is a path that does not look like siting a 1,000 MW campus.
- **A path that can sit next to the solar tax credit.** Carbon-free generation at the house, and fewer new commercial stations that communities do not want nearby.
- **Power at the house while the country is getting short.** Demand is rising faster than plants are being built. Rates have been climbing about 4% to 6% a year. A unit that covers the whole bill, not a slice of it, gets more valuable as that happens.
- **A clean end.** One lift, one replacement, processing included in the swap. Recycle and reuse where possible. What cannot be processed fits a paint-can receptacle after a 100-year life.

---

## Detriments

- **Early units will be expensive.** The first Stirling set is a real line (**$40,000 to $100,000** today). A complete solar-plus-battery job at the same house is the fair price comparison, not a panel-only ad. Solar’s first years were also expensive, and a federal credit (often 30%) brought the net cost down. The same kind of credit could apply here. Until it does, and until the vault and the engines are factory objects, the check is large.
- **The Stirling set wears out.** About every 10 to 15 years the five engines get swapped in the utility room. That later check is not in the simple-payback tables. The first set is.
- **It is not a weekend homeowner project.** It needs permits, a licensed set, a utility hookup, and extra piping if you want the heat in the house or in a greenhouse.
- **Not every house can use the heat indoors without added hardware.** Mini-splits and electric baseboard still get electricity. Whole-house space heat needs hot-water pipes, or the warmth goes to a greenhouse, showers, or a dedicated cooler.
- **In summer the house often does not want the warmth.** The station is still making heat while it makes electricity. If that heat is not sent to showers or a greenhouse, it has to be dumped on purpose through a small outdoor radiator (the same kind of box a heat pump already uses on the side of a house). If it is not dumped on purpose, it soaks into the soil around the vault. That does not heat the neighborhood, but that patch of yard runs warmer than the rest of the lot, and a vault close to the house can make the air conditioner work a little harder. August heat has to be planned. It is not unused warmth you can ignore.
- **The first approval does not exist yet.** Size-based approval is the plan. No regulator has issued that stamp.
- **The buried unit is a controlled object.** It is sealed and heavy on purpose. That is a benefit for the household and a responsibility for the people who make, move, and take it back.
- **End of life is specialized.** The swap includes removal and processing at a licensed facility. Leftover fuel is small (a paint-can class receptacle), but it is not handled like a stack of old solar panels.
- **Insurance, appraisal, and resale will have to treat this as a normal house feature.** Solar already went through that. Until this one is common, a buried vault plus a utility room will be a new line on a homeowners policy and a new note on an appraisal, the way solar panels once were.
- **Public comfort will take time.** A septic-tank-sized vault in the yard will need a clear, measurable safety story, not only a specification sheet.

## What this chapter covered

This is the idea at house scale, and only house scale: one family, one yard, one meter, compared to solar at that same meter. The later chapters are 3 Yard Safety, 4 The House, 5 The Vault, 6 The Approval, 7 The Swap, 8 Vault Spec, and 9 The Fuel. The claim here is that the physics is in the right size class, the house already has ways to use the heat, the owner’s life can stay simple, and the end of the story is a licensed crew and a crane.
