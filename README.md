# Backyard Generating Station

**Version 1.5** (24 September 2026)

A house should be able to make its own power, stay on when the wires are down, heat the rooms it already knows how to heat, and sell what it does not use.

The Backyard Generating Station is that idea as one buried appliance: a sealed vault about the size of a large septic tank, on a 10 by 10 foot patch of yard, with ten feet of soil on the lid. It is meant to make 5 to 10 kW of electricity around the clock, plus heat, from a very small nuclear core. About 30 kilograms (66 pounds) of fuel at first start.

Green means carbon-free electricity and heat at the house. The output does not stop when the sun goes down.

A licensed crew sets it. A licensed crew cranes it out. Nobody on the property opens it.

## If this is the only page you read

- **Power at the meter, sun or not.** 5 to 10 kW of steady electricity. At 8 kW, running most of the year, that is about 70,000 kWh. A typical house uses 10,000 to 12,000 kWh. A high-use house uses 20,000 to 35,000. The battery covers the evening spike. Extra can go to the grid the way solar already does.
- **Heat is the second product.** The core makes about 20 to 40 kW of heat along with the electricity. On many winter days that is enough for radiant floors and hot water, if the house already has a hot-water loop. The same warmth can run a greenhouse. In summer, heat the house does not want goes to showers, a greenhouse, or a small outdoor radiator.
- **The yard stays a yard.** If you stood on that patch of grass all day, every day, for a year, the extra dose is meant to be a few mrem. That is about the same as one cross-country flight. Ordinary background in the United States is about 300 mrem a year, so a year lived on that grass is about one percent of the dose you already get. An hour a day on a playset is a small fraction of that one flight. The vault is the shield. The ten feet of soil is extra. That dose is a design target. A transport calculation and a meter on the grass are still ahead of it.
- **The check, and what it depends on.** Once this is a factory object, and if it received the same 30% federal tax credit the United States gave rooftop solar in its early years, the all-in cost is about $50,000 to $90,000. At today's rates, simple payback is about 4 to 8 years if the utility pays near retail for the extra power, about 7 to 18 years at a low wholesale-style rate, and about 13 to 32 years if it pays nothing. A house with a $400 to $600 electric bill pays back faster. Early units are $130,000 to $260,000. The engines in the utility room are replaced about every 10 to 15 years. That later bill is not in the payback.
- **Nothing to run, and a planned end.** A propane tank or a heating-oil tank needs a delivery every couple of months. This unit does not. A solar installation puts fastener holes through the roof. This station does not. You do not operate it. Heat follows the thermostat the house already has. The utility meter shows what the house used and what went back to the grid. The buried unit is designed for 100 years without maintenance. At the end of that life a licensed crew lifts it out as one piece. What cannot be recycled leaves as a paint-can class receptacle at a licensed site.
- **What is in the book, and what is still ahead.** The book works the house-scale numbers: how much electricity, how much heat, how much fuel, and what it costs. Still ahead are a regulator's approval, a calculated dose at the grass, a 100-year test of the fuel and the heat pipes, and the highway tests for a vault with fuel in it. Until those exist, this is a concept, not a specification.

**This is a concept design basis.** It is not a DIY build, not construction drawings, and not a licensed nuclear plant, reactor, or appliance. No regulator has approved this design. Do not try to build it.
